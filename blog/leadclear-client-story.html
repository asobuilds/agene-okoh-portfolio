My First Paying Client — Building LeadClear Journal's Landing Page

The brief, the build, and why the business outcome mattered more than the code.

How it started

Anne Abiodun had a product — a 30-day leadership journal — and a paying audience on Selar. What she didn't have was a place to send people that made the product feel real. Selar's checkout page is functional but generic. Customers weren't connecting with the offer.

She needed a landing page that could do three things: communicate the value in under 10 seconds, look like the product was worth ₦3,500, and feed leads into her email funnel so she could follow up with people who didn't buy yet.

The constraint that shaped everything

She was already selling on Selar and running email through Systeme.io. I didn't want to replace either of those — that would mean she'd have to migrate data, re-learn a tool, and break whatever was already working.

So my rule became: the landing page plugs into her existing stack, it doesn't replace it. Two integrations were non-negotiable:

  • Selar — every "Buy Now" button links to her existing Selar checkout. Payments stay where her customers already trust them.
  • Systeme.io — a form at the bottom captures emails for anyone not ready to buy, dropping them directly into her existing funnel.

That decision meant zero learning curve for her. She kept her tools, gained a stronger front door.

What I built

  • Hero section — headline focused on the transformation, not the features. "30 days. Clear mind. Real action." The product name and price are right below, so the offer is unambiguous.
  • Product walkthrough — the Plan → Act → Reflect → Improve framework rendered visually, so buyers understand the structure before scrolling.
  • Audience segmentation — separate blocks for students, young professionals, entrepreneurs, and managers. Same product, four different reasons to buy.
  • FAQ section — pre-empted the four most common objections in her DMs: "Is it printable?" "Is it physical or digital?" "Do I need experience?" "How long does shipping take?"
  • Selar CTA buttons — repeated at four scroll depths, each linking to her live checkout link.
  • Systeme.io capture form — for the "interested but not ready" visitor, feeding her lead list.

Technical decisions

Static HTML/CSS/JS. No framework, no build step. Loads in under a second on Nigerian 4G, which matters when a chunk of your audience is on mobile data.

Mobile-first CSS. Over 70% of her traffic comes from phones. Every section is tested at 375px width first, then scaled up.

Vercel for deployment. Free, global CDN, HTTPS included, and she gets a live URL she can share instantly. Any future edit I make goes live in under 10 seconds.

No analytics bloat. Just Vercel Analytics — lightweight, privacy-respecting, and gives her the two numbers that matter: visitors and CTA clicks.

The moment it clicked

When I sent her the live preview link, her response was immediate:

"This is really nice honestly. Beautiful — just exactly what I had in mind and even better. God bless you. I will be inviting you to attend my webinars."

That last line is the part that mattered most. Not the compliment — the invitation. She's a business owner with an audience. When she brings me into her webinars, she's putting her reputation behind my work. That's the trust signal that no portfolio piece can manufacture.

What I'd do differently

I'd have added A/B testing from day one. Two versions of the hero headline, split 50/50 for the first two weeks. Right now we're guessing at what converts, and Anne's audience is small enough that every visitor's opinion matters. That's the next add.

I'd also set up a lightweight dashboard for her — visitors, CTA clicks, email captures — so she can see the funnel working without asking me to check.

The lesson

Technical excellence gets the job done. Understanding the business behind the brief is what makes the client come back. Anne didn't hire me because I know React or Go or Python. She hired me because I asked how her funnel worked before I wrote a single line of HTML — and then built something that plugged into it instead of fighting it.

For any freelancer starting out: don't sell code. Sell the outcome. The code is how you deliver it.

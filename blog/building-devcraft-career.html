How I Built DevCraft Career

A CV builder that scans the internet for jobs and sends users the apply link — built because job hunting is broken.

The problem I kept seeing

Job hunting is a fragmented mess. You build a CV on one site, search for jobs on another, copy-paste your details into a third, and track everything in a spreadsheet. By the time you've applied to ten roles, you've spent more time on logistics than on actually pitching yourself.

I built DevCraft Career to compress that loop into one place: build your CV → get matched → click apply. Everything else is friction I wanted to remove.

What it does

  • CV builder. Fill in your details, pick a template (with or without photo), attach certificates. The system renders a professional CV.
  • Job scanner. The system queries job sources across private, government, and freelance listings, then matches against your profile.
  • Alerts with apply links. When there's a match, you get a notification with the direct application link — no middle step.
  • Free for users. No paywall on the CV builder or the matcher.

The architecture I landed on

Frontend is React, deployed on Vercel. It handles the CV builder UI, template rendering, and the notification feed.

Backend is Python — the job scanner and matcher live here. Every few hours a scheduled job hits the APIs, normalizes results, and stores them. Matching runs on a simple scoring function: skill overlap, experience level, location, and recency.

Persistence is a lightweight store — enough to remember who's subscribed to what. I deliberately kept it simple: no auth wall for browsing, no friction to try it.

Decisions I'd defend, and one I'd revisit

Defensible: keeping the CV builder client-side until the user exports. There's no server round-trip while they edit. It's fast, private, and cheap to run.

Defensible: separating the scanner into its own service. Job data changes constantly, and I didn't want the API to bloat every time I added a new source.

Revisit: I leaned on a single scheduler for the scan cycle. It works, but if DevCraft grows, I'd move to a queue-based worker pool so a slow source doesn't block the rest.

What users are telling me

The feedback loop has been the best part. One user built their CV in under 10 minutes and got matched to a remote frontend role within a week. Another — a data analyst — said the alerts "actually match my skills". The bar is: don't send noise.

What's next

Three things I'm actively working on:

  • Better matching. The current scorer is heuristic. I want to layer in a lightweight embedding model over CV + job description text.
  • Application tracking. A private dashboard where users can mark applications as "applied", "interview", "offer" — so the whole loop lives in one place.
  • Interview prep. Once matched, surface common questions for the role.

The bigger idea

DevCraft Career is really a bet that the job search is a systems problem, not a search problem. The listings already exist everywhere. What's missing is a pipeline that connects a specific person to the specific roles they can actually win — and gives them the shortest path to apply.
